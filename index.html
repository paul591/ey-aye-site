One place to ask.

Your business already knows the answer to most questions. The trouble is the answer's split across five logins. Bookings in one place, invoices in another, the customer's last email somewhere else, the stock in a spreadsheet.

We join those up so an AI can see all of them at once, safely, read-only where it should be. Then you ask it like you'd ask a good office manager: "Who's in this week that still owes us?" "What did we quote the Smiths last time?" "Which lines haven't sold since spring?" Once that's in place, the automation builds on top of it: the morning brief, the quote that writes itself, the chase that goes out on Friday.

Engineer by trade. Business builder by habit.

Ey-aye is Paul Lofthouse. I've launched and run brands of my own. Bodyworks by Corey, the massage and lymphatic drainage practice in Harrogate, is my build from the ground up: Corey is the therapist, and I run the brand, the bookings, the marketing and everything behind the front desk. Purple Helmet, the motorsport apparel brand, is the same story: brand, store, community, back end.

Underneath that I'm an engineer. Twenty years of engine control units, dynamometers and race-car data systems, and lately the software that lets AI talk to all of it. My day job is with an automotive software company whose tools sit inside major car makers' engineering teams, and the biggest AI product I've shipped is theirs: an assistant that lets engineers ask their calibration platform questions in plain English. The same idea runs my own race-data system, which you can see.

So when I say something's worth doing, it's because I've had to make it pay in a business I own, not because it looked good in a slide deck. And if it's worth building, I build it myself.
