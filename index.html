Everyone's worried about missing out on AI. Nobody's sure where it goes.

That's not your fault. The adverts don't help. Every other post on Facebook is a "does it all for you" system for £200 a month, and most of them are a thin wrapper round the same ChatGPT you could use yourself for a tenner.

Here's the honest order of things. Most businesses get the first big win from using the tools they already pay for properly. Some need those tools plugged into their own systems. A few need something built. We'll tell you which you are, and we won't sell you the third one if the first one does the job.

Give your business one place to ask.

Your business already knows the answer to most questions. The trouble is the answer's split across five logins. Bookings in one place, invoices in another, the customer's last email somewhere else, the stock in a spreadsheet.

We join those up so an AI can see all of them at once, safely, read-only where it should be. Then you ask it like you'd ask a good office manager: "Who's in this week that still owes us?" "What did we quote the Smiths last time?" "Which lines haven't sold since spring?" Once that's in place, the automation builds on top of it: the morning brief, the quote that writes itself, the chase that goes out on Friday.

Engineer by trade. Business builder by habit.

Ey-aye is Paul Lofthouse. I've launched and run brands of my own. Bodyworks by Corey is the massage and lymphatic drainage practice in Harrogate that Corey founded: she's the therapist, and I launched the brand and run the bookings, the marketing and everything behind the front desk. Her name for the job is "back-end boy". Purple Helmet, the motorsport apparel brand, is mine end to end: brand, store, community, back end.

Underneath that I'm a software architect, thirty years in: engine control units, dynamometers, race-car data systems, and lately the software that lets AI talk to all of it. I've built with these models from the inside, not just typed into them, so I know what they're good at and where they bluff. My day job is with an automotive software company whose tools sit inside major car makers' engineering teams, and the biggest AI product I've shipped is theirs: an assistant that lets engineers ask their calibration platform questions in plain English. The same idea runs my own race-data system, which you can see.

So when I say something's worth doing, it's because I've had to make it pay in a business I own, not because it looked good in a slide deck. And if it's worth building, I build it myself.
